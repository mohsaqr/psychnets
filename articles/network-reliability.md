# Edge-weight stability and split-half reliability

Can the strongest edges and most prominent nodes in a psychological
network be trusted to remain similar in another sample? Consider a
network of self-efficacy, learning strategies and test anxiety, in which
the constructs are nodes and their estimated associations are edges. In
a partial correlation network, each edge describes an association
adjusted for the other variables, while centrality measures summarise
aspects of each node’s position. These tools allow researchers to
explore multivariate patterns rather than examine every construct in
isolation (Borsboom et al., 2021). Yet the interpretation of edge
weights and centrality depends on the estimation method and available
data, making stability and reliability important considerations
(Isvoranu & Epskamp, 2023).

Network methods have been used in education to study the relationships
among learning processes and how these relationships vary across
contexts and time. Saqr et al. (2024) introduce psychological networks
as an approach to analysing learning and complex learning processes.
Applications include networks of students’ monitoring events across
phases of regulation (Malmberg et al., 2022) and the temporal dynamics
of self-regulation during academic writing (Saqr et al., 2021). These
studies illustrate the value of examining patterns of relationships, but
an apparent connection or prominent node in an estimated network should
not automatically be treated as a reproducible finding.

A network fitted to one sample will generally differ from a network
fitted to another sample from the same population. Consequently, applied
researchers need to assess uncertainty in individual edge weights,
whether nodes can be distinguished in centrality, and how much the
network changes when observations are omitted or divided into groups.
Resampling provides an empirical way to investigate these questions by
repeatedly estimating networks from the available observations (Epskamp
et al., 2018). Assessing and clearly reporting such uncertainty is also
part of good practice in cross-sectional network analysis (Burger et
al., 2023). These checks describe the robustness of an estimated
network; they do not establish causal relationships or guarantee
replication in a new population.

This article demonstrates five complementary diagnostics in `psychnets`:
bootstrap intervals for edges and centrality, bootstrap comparisons of
nodes, case-dropping stability of centrality, case-dropping reliability
of edge weights, and split-half reliability. The worked example uses
questionnaire responses generated by a large language model, rather than
observations of students, and therefore illustrates methods rather than
substantive findings about learners. For each diagnostic, we explain the
question it addresses, show the relevant output, and discuss what
researchers can reasonably report. The functions use a consistent
network-estimation interface, allowing the same workflow to be used with
other estimators.

``` r

library(psychnets)
set.seed(1)
```

Because the procedures involve random resampling, setting the seed makes
the illustrative results reproducible.

## Data

`SRL_Claude` contains 300 sets of responses to the Motivated Strategies
for Learning Questionnaire generated by the large language model Claude
for a study of responses to psychological surveys (Vogelsmeier et al.,
2025). The model-generated responses are unusually internally consistent
and produce several large estimated edges. They make the numerical
examples easy to inspect, but do not provide evidence about
relationships among constructs in real students.

- **Nodes:** five construct scores on a scale from 1 to 7: `CSU`
  (cognitive strategy use), `IV` (intrinsic value), `SE`
  (self-efficacy), `SR` (self-regulation) and `TA` (test anxiety).
- **Observations:** one row per respondent; the data are complete.

## How precise is each edge?

[`net_boot()`](https://pak.dynasite.org/psychnets/reference/net_boot.md)
draws samples of observations with replacement, estimates a network for
each sample and summarises the resulting edge weights. The output
provides each observed weight, the bootstrap mean, a 95% percentile
interval and the proportion of resamples in which the edge has a
non-zero estimate. The `significant` flag indicates that the percentile
interval excludes zero; it should not be read as proof of an underlying
relationship. For substantive research, at least 1,000 bootstrap samples
provide a more useful basis for assessing precision. Here, 200 resamples
keep the demonstration manageable.

``` r

b <- net_boot(SRL_Claude, method = "glasso", n_boot = 200, cores = 1)
b
```

Eight of the ten edges have percentile intervals that exclude zero, and

those edges. The plot presents the edge estimates alongside their

## How precise is each node’s centrality?

The bootstrap samples also provide intervals for node centrality.
Strength is the sum of the absolute weights of a node’s edges, whereas
expected influence sums signed edge weights. This distinction matters
when the network contains both positive and negative associations.


Self-regulation (`SR`) has the highest observed strength, 1.95, with a
bootstrap interval from 1.83 to 2.03. This interval does not overlap the
interval of any other node. Intrinsic value (`IV`) has the lowest
observed strength, 0.99, with an interval from 0.83 to 1.27. The
intervals for cognitive strategy use, self-efficacy and test anxiety
overlap, so their ranking cannot be inferred from separate intervals
alone. Expected influence answers a different question: self-efficacy
and test anxiety have negative values (-0.37 and -0.33), reflecting the
balance of their signed edges. The next analysis compares strengths
directly.

## Which nodes can be ranked?

[`difference_test()`](https://pak.dynasite.org/psychnets/reference/difference_test.md)
compares the centralities of pairs of nodes or the weights of pairs of
edges. It calculates each difference across the existing bootstrap
samples, then reports its percentile interval and a two-sided bootstrap
p-value. The stored samples from
[`net_boot()`](https://pak.dynasite.org/psychnets/reference/net_boot.md)
are reused rather than fitted again. For the ten pairwise strength
comparisons, `p_adjust = "BH"` applies a false discovery rate adjustment
to the p-values (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995). An interval containing
zero indicates that the bootstrap differences do not clearly distinguish
the pair.

``` r

difference_test(b, type = "strength", p_adjust = "BH")
#>    item1 item2    value1    value2    obs_diff        lower       upper
#> 1    CSU    IV 1.4794795 0.9920086  0.48747089  0.241546367  0.66372411
```

Nine of the ten comparisons have intervals excluding zero and are
flagged as significant in the output. Self-regulation has greater
strength than every other node, with adjusted p-values reported as 0 for
these comparisons at the available bootstrap resolution. Cognitive
strategy use also exceeds self-efficacy and test anxiety (adjusted p =
0.044 for each comparison). The only pair not distinguished is
self-efficacy and test anxiety: their observed difference is -0.12, with
an interval from -0.33 to 0.08 and an adjusted p-value of 0.26. Thus,
self-regulation can be placed first and intrinsic value last in this
example, but the evidence does not establish an ordering between
self-efficacy and test anxiety.

## Is the centrality ranking stable when cases are dropped?

[`net_stability()`](https://pak.dynasite.org/psychnets/reference/net_stability.md)
re-estimates the network on random subsets that drop an increasing
proportion of the respondents and correlates the centralities of each
subset with those of the full sample. The correlation-stability
coefficient (CS-coefficient) is the largest proportion of cases that can
be dropped while the correlation stays at or above 0.7 in at least 95%
of the subsets (Epskamp et al., 2018).

``` r

st <- net_stability(SRL_Claude, method = "glasso", iter = 100)
st
#> <psychnet_stability> glasso, 100 subsets/proportion
#>   CS-coefficient (pearson cor > 0.70 in > 95% of subsets):
#>     strength             0.70
#>     expected_influence   0.90
```

The reported CS-coefficients are 0.70 for strength and 0.90 for expected
influence. Under the diagnostic’s criterion, a drop proportion of 0.70
retains correlations of at least 0.7 with full-sample strength in at
least 95% of the sampled subsets. Epskamp et al. (2018) recommend a
coefficient above 0.25, preferably above 0.5, for interpreting
centrality. Both estimates comfortably exceed these reference values.
This supports the stability of the centrality pattern in the example,
although it does not resolve every pairwise difference. The plot
displays mean correlations by drop proportion, with a band showing one
standard deviation and a horizontal threshold at 0.7:

``` r

plot(st)
```

![](network-reliability_files/figure-html/stability-plot-1.png)

full sample through their correlation and through the mean, median and

## Does the network replicate across halves of the sample?

[`net_split_reliability()`](https://pak.dynasite.org/psychnets/reference/net_split_reliability.md)
divides the sample at random into two halves, estimates a network on
each, and compares the two sets of edges, repeating the split many
times. The result has one row per comparison measure, with its mean,
standard deviation and the 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles across splits.

``` r

rel <- net_split_reliability(SRL_Claude, method = "glasso", iter = 100)
rel
#> # split-half reliability: glasso | 100 iterations (50/50 split)
#>           metric       mean         sd      lower     upper n_valid
#> 1   mean_abs_dev 0.06406132 0.02201012 0.03319670 0.1092927     100
#> 2 median_abs_dev 0.05674286 0.02297601 0.02204948 0.1086049     100
#> 3    correlation 0.98243129 0.01208858 0.95313106 0.9960735     100
#> 4    max_abs_dev 0.14177087 0.05219793 0.06749545 0.2635560     100
```

Across 100 random 50/50 splits, the mean correlation between the two
sets of edge weights is 0.982. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the
correlations are 0.953 and 0.996. Each half contains 150 observations,
but the halves are repeatedly drawn from the same dataset, not from two
new independent studies. The mean absolute edge-weight deviation across
splits is 0.064, and the mean of the maximum absolute deviations is
0.142. These results indicate strong agreement in the overall edge
pattern within this dataset, alongside some variation in particular edge
weights; they do not by themselves demonstrate external replication.

``` r

plot(rel)
```

![](network-reliability_files/figure-html/reliability-plot-1.png)

## When to use which diagnostic

| Question | Verb | Read the result with |
|----|----|----|
| How precise is each edge and centrality? | [`net_boot()`](https://pak.dynasite.org/psychnets/reference/net_boot.md) | `as.data.frame(b)`, `as.data.frame(b, what = "centrality")`, [`plot()`](https://rdrr.io/r/graphics/plot.default.html) |
| Do two edges or two nodes differ? | [`difference_test()`](https://pak.dynasite.org/psychnets/reference/difference_test.md) | the returned table, [`plot()`](https://rdrr.io/r/graphics/plot.default.html) |
| Is the centrality order stable when cases are dropped? | [`net_stability()`](https://pak.dynasite.org/psychnets/reference/net_stability.md) | `as.data.frame(st, what = "cs")`, `as.data.frame(st)`, [`plot()`](https://rdrr.io/r/graphics/plot.default.html) |
| Is the edge structure stable when cases are dropped? | [`net_casedrop_reliability()`](https://pak.dynasite.org/psychnets/reference/net_casedrop_reliability.md) | the returned table, [`plot()`](https://rdrr.io/r/graphics/plot.default.html) |
| Does the network replicate across halves? | [`net_split_reliability()`](https://pak.dynasite.org/psychnets/reference/net_split_reliability.md) | the returned table, [`plot()`](https://rdrr.io/r/graphics/plot.default.html) |

- Report bootstrap intervals for edges of substantive interest. Treat an
  interval containing zero as insufficient evidence to interpret that
  edge confidently, rather than as proof that the relationship is
  absent.
- Use pairwise difference tests alongside case-dropping stability when
  discussing node rankings. A CS-coefficient below 0.25 warrants
  particular caution about centrality interpretation.
- Distinguish uncertainty in a particular edge from agreement in the
  network as a whole. Here, high case-dropping and split-half
  correlations coexist with two edge intervals that include zero.
- These procedures assume independent observations. For repeated
  observations from the same individuals, resampling must respect the
  dependence between observations from each individual.
